Build a computer

A desktop computer is nine kinds of parts that plug into each other. Pull it apart below, then pick a part to see what it does, what its numbers mean, and what it connects to.

The parts

Processor

Power 65–170 W

CPU Executes program instructions: the arithmetic, logic and control at the core of the system.

Socket Must match the motherboard exactly. The shape and contact layout of the processor's underside. It must match the motherboard's socket exactly. This is the first thing to check, and the most common mistake. Examples: AM5, LGA 1851.
AM5
Cores and threads More cores do more jobs at once. Cores are separate workers. A thread is a stream of work a core keeps going; some cores handle two at once. More cores help most with work that splits up. Examples: 8 cores, 16 threads.
8 cores
Clock speed Higher is faster, within one generation. How many steps a core takes per second, in billions. Boost is the top speed for short bursts while the processor is cool enough. Only compare clock speeds within the same generation: newer designs do more per step. Examples: 4.2 GHz base, up to 5.4 GHz boost.
5.4 GHz
Power Sets how strong the cooler and power supply must be. Roughly how much heat the processor gives off under steady load, in watts. Makers call it TDP or base power, and some list a higher short-term figure too. The cooler has to remove it and the power supply has to provide it. Examples: 65 W, 120 W.
120 W
Cache Fast memory on the chip; more helps some games. A small store of very fast memory on the chip itself. A bigger cache means fewer trips out to the RAM, which helps some games noticeably. Examples: 32 MB.
32 MB
Memory support Which kind of RAM it can use. Which generation of RAM the processor can drive. The motherboard settles the final choice: a board has DDR4 slots or DDR5 slots, never both. Examples: DDR5 only.
DDR5
Built-in graphics Can it show a picture without a graphics card? Whether the processor can drive a monitor on its own, through the motherboard's video ports. Examples: yes / no.
Yes

Motherboard

Size ATX

Motherboard Main circuit board: routes power and data between every component; its chipset and firmware run the platform.

Socket Must match the motherboard exactly. Which processors fit. It must match the processor exactly. Examples: AM5, LGA 1851.
AM5
Chipset Sets how many extra ports and features you get. Decides how many extra slots and ports the board has, and features such as overclocking. Each processor maker names its own chipset families. Examples: a B- or Z-series chipset.
B-series
Form factor Its size: the case has to fit it. The board's size and screw-hole layout. ATX is 305 × 244 mm, Micro-ATX up to 244 × 244 mm, Mini-ITX 170 × 170 mm. The case must list it. Examples: ATX, Micro-ATX, Mini-ITX.
ATX
Memory How many RAM sticks fit, and which kind. How many memory sticks fit and which generation. Mini-ITX boards usually have two slots. Examples: 4 slots, DDR5.
4 × DDR5
M.2 slots and SATA ports How many drives you can connect. Where drives connect. M.2 drives plug straight into the board; SATA drives use a cable. Examples: 3 × M.2, 4 × SATA.
3 × M.2
Expansion slots Where the graphics card and add-in cards go. Long slots for a graphics card and other add-in cards. The top long slot is usually the one wired to the processor. Examples: 1 × PCIe 5.0 x16.
PCIe x16
Back panel The ports on the back of the computer. The ports at the back of the computer. The board's video outputs only work with a processor that has built-in graphics. Examples: USB, network, Wi-Fi, video out.
USB, Wi-Fi

Memory

Typical 16–64 GB

RAM Volatile working memory: holds running programs and their data for the CPU at high speed.

Type DDR4 or DDR5: must match the motherboard. The memory generation. The two have their notch in different places, so a stick physically fits only its own kind of slot. The motherboard decides which one you need. Examples: DDR4 or DDR5.
DDR5
Capacity How much fits at once. More means more open at once. How much the kit holds in total, and how it's split across sticks. Examples: 32 GB (2 × 16 GB).
32 GB
Kit size Buy a matched pair for full speed. Memory works fastest in pairs (dual channel). Buy the sticks as one kit, so they're matched, and put them in the slots the board's manual names, often the second and fourth. Examples: 2 sticks.
2 sticks
Speed and latency Higher speed and lower CL are faster. How fast data moves, and how long each request waits. Higher speed and lower CL are faster. The rated speed usually needs the kit's memory profile turned on in the BIOS; without it, the memory runs at a slower default. Examples: 6000 MT/s, CL30.
6000 MT/s
Height Tall sticks can block a big air cooler. Tall heat spreaders can get in the way of a large air cooler that overhangs the memory slots. Examples: 35 mm.
35 mm

Storage

Typical 1–4 TB

Drive Non-volatile storage for the operating system, programs and files.

Interface NVMe is the fast one. How the drive talks to the board. NVMe uses fast PCIe lanes; SATA is an older, slower link. Examples: NVMe or SATA.
NVMe
Capacity How much it holds. How much it holds. Makers count 1 TB as 1000 GB, while computers count in powers of two, so a 2 TB drive shows up as about 1.8 TB. Examples: 2 TB.
2 TB
PCIe generation Newer is faster, and works in older slots. The speed of the link an NVMe drive uses. A newer drive works in an older slot, at the slot's speed. Examples: Gen 4, Gen 5.
Gen 4
Size code The drive's size: 2280 is the usual one. For M.2 drives: 22 mm wide, 80 mm long. Most boards take 2280; some slots also take shorter or longer drives. Examples: 2280.
2280
Endurance How much it can write before wearing out. Rarely a worry. How much data the maker expects can be written before the drive wears out. Normal use takes years to get near it. Examples: 1,200 TBW.
1,200 TBW

Video memory More helps at high resolutions and detail. Memory on the card itself, for textures and frames. Higher resolutions and detail settings need more. Examples: 12 GB.
12 GB
Length Must fit inside the case. Cards vary a lot in length. The case lists the longest card it can hold. Examples: 242 mm, 330 mm.
300 mm
Thickness How many slots wide it is. How many expansion-slot widths the card covers. Small cases may have room for only two or three. Examples: 2 slots, 3.1 slots.
2 slots
Power Usually the biggest power user in the computer. How much power the card draws under load. Usually the biggest draw in the whole computer. Examples: 250 W.
250 W
Recommended power supply The power supply size the card maker suggests. The card maker's suggestion for the whole computer's power supply, with headroom built in. Examples: 650 W.
650 W

Case fans

Size 120 mm

Fan Exchange the air inside the case: cool air in, heated air out.

Size Frame width: bigger is usually quieter. The width of the fan's square frame. A bigger fan moves the same air at a lower speed, so it's quieter. The case lists which sizes fit where. Examples: 120 mm, 140 mm.
120 mm
Airflow How much air it moves. How much air it moves, in cubic feet per minute, with nothing in its way. Higher moves more. Examples: 60 CFM.
60 CFM
Static pressure How well it pushes through radiators and filters. How hard it can push air through something dense, like a radiator or a dust filter. Fans on a radiator need more of it. Examples: 2.0 mm H2O.
2.0 mm H2O
Speed Set by the motherboard from temperatures. How fast it spins, in revolutions per minute. The motherboard raises and lowers it with temperature. Examples: 400–1600 RPM.
1600 RPM
Connector 4-pin lets the board control speed precisely. A 4-pin (PWM) fan lets the board set its speed precisely; a 3-pin fan is slowed down by lowering its voltage. Both plug into the board's fan headers. Examples: 4-pin PWM or 3-pin.
4-pin PWM
Noise Lower is quieter. How loud it is at full speed. About 10 dBA more sounds roughly twice as loud. Examples: 25 dBA.
25 dBA
